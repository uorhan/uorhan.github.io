First of all, welcome everyone.  
There have been some shifts in the schedule. I had planned everything according to the time that was originally given to me, and I only found out about the change when I arrived here. Anyway…

Let me start with the cover image I prepared. Most of you have probably seen the man in this painting:  
Osman Hamdi Bey’s famous work *The Tortoise Trainer*. I turned this painting into a kind of “hybrid” using new AI tools. Instead of the tortoises at the bottom, I placed the logos of the big AI companies we all know, and I gave the scene an office-like atmosphere. If you look carefully, it even seems as if he has a hidden stick in his pocket; that’s probably just a small glitch of the model.

When I say **“The Profession of the Future: The AI Tamer,”** I don’t want to be misunderstood. The “tamer” here is not someone literally cracking a whip; it means the person who uses AI correctly, ethically, and efficiently. In other words, the profession of the future is not the one that fights with AI, but the person who can guide it in the right direction.

Now, while our time is limited, let me start with a tough question right away:

> Today, which profession has the lowest risk of unemployment and the highest salary?

Of course, there isn’t a single right answer. Some will think of medicine, some of computer engineering, software development, or maybe other professions… I’ll ask this question again at the end of the talk and we’ll see if your answers change.

Let me briefly introduce myself. Even though I’m currently introduced as the Chair of the Computer Engineering Department, that’s just the visible tip of the iceberg. My “love story” with computers actually began in the second year of high school.

I got a computer as a report-card gift. Along with it came a magazine. There was some code in it that I didn’t understand at the time. Turned out it was a simple BASIC program. Back then, when we thought of a computer, we thought of games, but there weren’t really proper games around; even pressing keys on the keyboard was fun for me. Out of curiosity, I typed in the code from the magazine. When I typed in some English words I didn’t understand, the computer started reacting. My English wasn’t very good back then either—think of the weak English education you get in a typical public school. But when I typed `PRINT`, something appeared on the screen.

Then I asked myself: “I wonder if it reacts to other words in the dictionary as well?” I tried. Most of them didn’t work, but a few did. That’s when I realized: I type something into the computer, and it responds to me. That interaction completely fascinated me.

Up until the second year of high school, it was the classic Turkish scenario: “You’re going to be a doctor.” I was raised to become a doctor too. In the second year I changed my mind; when applying to university, I filled in all my choices with only computer engineering programs. Looking back now, I realize this: my true love wasn’t actually “the computer” itself; it was making machines do things and seeing the results instantly.

In my first year studying computer engineering, the programs I wrote were very simple. The internet was just starting to enter our lives. The upperclassmen were asking, “Who is this freshman who keeps going online all the time?” Then in the second semester of my first year, I learned more programming and discovered what they called “artificial intelligence.” I had some old games I played: Turkish checkers and a small game I called “Mini Four.” I wrote code so I could play them against the computer. Of course, my programming knowledge back then was limited. What I had built was a rule-based system—what we call an expert system, where we explicitly tell the machine what to do step by step.

By the way, most of the systems we talk about today still aren’t as “human-like” as people think. The progress is incredible, but we’re still nowhere near human intelligence.

Anyway, let’s look at things a bit chronologically:

Around 1996–97, we heard about IBM’s Deep Blue beating Garry Kasparov. Then in 2016, using much more advanced methods, Google’s AlphaGo beat the world Go champion. The stage is always the same: big companies, big shows.

When I heard about these, I said, “Wow, AI is really powerful,” but I didn’t sit there thinking, “What will happen to my profession in the future? Will I be unemployed?” I was simply interested, and that interest made me want to spend more time in that area, that’s all.

From the 2000s up until the pandemic, I took part in many projects that involved AI in one way or another. Some of them were commercialized, some became academic outputs, and some took shape as TUBITAK or industry projects. I built a program that plays backgammon, we developed computer vision systems that still run in factories; we have systems still working in big plants like DİMES and Kastamonu Entegre.

Then the pandemic came; like everyone else, I found myself stuck at home. I grew tomatoes, baked bread… And I also started imagining a world and wrote a novel on top of that. That’s how *Artificial World Colony* was born. Eventually it turned into a book sold on Amazon. When you write science fiction, your dreams get bigger and you start wondering: “Could this one day turn into a Hollywood movie?”

Around that time, I noticed something else: I’m dreaming, but the people in Silicon Valley are not exactly idle either. It’s as if they’ve built a “second Hollywood.” The work they do is impressive, I have no objection there; but their claims are huge.

In 2017, Meta (then called Facebook) ran a chatbot experiment. They told a story along the lines of: “Two bots started talking to each other, things got out of control, and thankfully our engineers noticed and shut it down in time, saving humanity.” At that time, there were no large language models like today, nor such advanced infrastructure. But the story itself was very “Hollywood.”

Then in 2020, OpenAI introduced GPT-3 in an interview with *The Guardian*, using language that felt more like an advertisement: dramatic lines like, “I’m not planning to wipe out humanity—for now.” Today we all use GPT variants. Are they powerful? Yes. But they still don’t come close to human intelligence. Because we know what’s happening in the background: heavy statistics, massive amounts of data, and algorithms that mostly imitate the memorization side of intelligence.

The essence of it is this: they didn’t fundamentally change the algorithm of intelligence; they scaled up the data. They continuously generate new training data from wrong answers, user feedback, and texts on the internet, and keep updating the model.

I’ve tested this many times. I asked Siri, Gemini, and later GPT to tell me jokes. The current GPT version is a bit better, but it still creates jokes by acting like a system that “fills the details we give it into a ready-made template.” It turns the *Tortoise Trainer* example I showed at the beginning into a joke in exactly the same way: it takes fragments from prompts and concepts I had previously given it, and inserts them into a pre-existing joke structure. In other words, what we have is a staged illusion of “human-like intelligence.”

Now, let’s come back to our main topic: careers and unemployment…

Every new invention in history has inevitably upset some people. When the printing press appeared, calligraphers worried: “We’re out of work.” But handwritten books didn’t disappear overnight; demand decreased, population increased, and the work slowly transformed rather than vanishing.

During the Industrial Revolution, machines transformed the jobs of traditional masters, but they also created new professions. When cars appeared, carriage drivers struggled; but then a huge ecosystem emerged: the automotive industry, roads, logistics, insurance, tourism, and more. Every solution brought at least one new problem, and every new problem turned into a new field of work.

With AI, the situation isn’t fundamentally different; it just feels more frightening because the change is faster and more visible.

In my view, the key point is this:  
AI will transform many jobs and will largely automate some tasks. But this won’t be a “we woke up one morning and everyone was unemployed” scenario. Young people’s career choices, how the education system adapts, regulation, and data collection processes will soften the impact of this transformation.

Here I especially want to emphasize the topic of data. For years, we ignored how important proper data collection is in health, law, education, and the public sector. Today, when you say “Let’s make a revolution in healthcare with AI,” the first thing you need is clean, labeled, consistent, and up-to-date data. The same goes for law, agriculture, foreign trade—everywhere.

That’s why I particularly want to stress this:  
The profession of the future is not just “AI engineer”;  
it’s being a **domain expert who can work in coordination with AI**.

If you are a doctor, a lawyer, a psychologist, a teacher, or a foreign trade specialist… and you know how data should be collected, cleaned, and annotated in your field, then you will be the person who feeds the AI of that domain. That will be a very valuable role in the coming years.

Let me briefly touch on some frequently asked questions.

**Will AI replace doctors?**  
Yes, to a degree. In radiology and imaging, AI’s role is already growing. But the point where we hand over full responsibility is still far away. The number of specialists may decrease over time, roles may shift; but a scenario where “all doctors suddenly lose their jobs” is not realistic.

**Will AI replace lawyers?**  
Yes, in part. A large portion of simple, standard cases could potentially be resolved from home using a well-designed national legal assistant. But complex cases, negotiations, strategy, and the human and ethical dimensions will remain with humans for a long time.

**Will AI replace teachers?**  
Yes, in terms of delivering information, that part can largely be automated. Even today, GPT organizes a large part of my lecture notes better than I do. But getting to know the student, motivating them, providing pedagogical support, dealing with their psychology… that’s the human domain. Maybe in the future, teachers will evolve from “people who deliver information” into “learning coaches.”

**Will AI replace software developers?**  
For routine and repetitive coding tasks, yes. It greatly speeds up things like “write this function” or “connect this API.” This also allows people from other fields to build simple applications. But the part that involves solving unsolved problems for the first time, designing architecture, abstracting, and optimizing is still the developer’s job.

Now let’s talk about the “laziness” question: do people who use AI tools a lot really become lazy?

If you never learn the basic skills and only rely on “ready-made answers,” your mental muscles will weaken. But your brain will automatically memorize the information you use frequently and that lies at the core of your work. Rarely used detailed information doesn’t need to clutter your mind; it’s reasonable to pull that from tools when needed.

New approaches are also emerging in education. All over the world, many universities are rethinking assignments like take-home essays, reports, and term papers. Because students are having ChatGPT write the text and handing it in without even reading it. The solution: change the assignment format—more in-class work, oral presentations, project-based assessments, practical exams…

Let me also briefly touch on language.  
Some people say, “We have translation tools now, there’s no need to learn English.” That’s partly true, partly not. Maybe for reading academic papers, you won’t *always* need C1-level English as strictly as before. But in a job interview, a meeting, a conference, a debate, you can’t say, “Hang on a second, let me type what I want to say into a translation app first.”

Besides, language is not just vocabulary and grammar; it’s culture, humor, and a way of thinking. Without understanding that, it’s hard to operate globally.

Now let’s briefly look at the current job landscape:  
Statistics show that logistics and e-commerce–based jobs are currently among the largest sources of employment. Delivery, couriers, warehouses, stock management, supply chains—together they form a huge sector. On top of that, sales, customer relations, and the broader service sector are still very large.

On the white-collar side, IT and software will remain important for a long time. But that will also transform internally: not just “people who write code,” but people who understand the domain problem, work with data, and integrate AI will stand out.

Here, the difference in perspective between generations is also important.  
Someone who has reached a certain age and has a stable career can say, “Nothing will really change before I retire.” That’s a reasonable calculation for them. But a new graduate doesn’t have that luxury. They will have to live through the next 30–40 years of transformation.

Now we come to the most critical message:  
**Don’t let circumstances force you to give up the love of your life.**

I fell in love with computers in my second year of high school. Then AI caught my interest, and eventually I wrote science fiction on top of that. Don’t give up on your passions. But also learn how to “fine-tune” them.

In machine learning, fine-tuning means adjusting a pre-trained model with new data. In your career, it’s similar:  
You keep your big goal, but you make small adjustments as the road twists and turns. You might say, “I want to be a doctor,” but then optimize your specialty choice, how you use technology, and maybe your research direction. Or you might say, “I want to be an engineer,” and shape your own combination of data, AI, and domain expertise.

Finally, let’s return to the question at the beginning of this talk:

> In tomorrow’s world, which profession will have the lowest risk of unemployment and the highest salary?

I don’t have a single, definite answer.  
But I’m quite sure of this:  
those who are truly good in their field,  
who stay open to learning,  
who see AI not as a frightening rival but as a smart tool,  
who take both data and people seriously,  
and who can “fine-tune” their passions—  
they will be the ones ahead.

Thank you for listening.
